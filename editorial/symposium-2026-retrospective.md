# Discovering and Inventing New Nature

*A retrospective on the 2026 Protocol Symposium*

For five days in late September, a large and scattered crowd stared at a flat rectangle together and tried to work out what kind of world they are living in. The [2026 Protocol Symposium](https://protocol-institute.org/events/protocol-symposium-2026/) ran September 21–25, online: two days of workshops, then three days and roughly fifty talks, panels, special sessions and an open mic. The theme was **new nature**: the idea that the technological environment we have built, made of protocols, infrastructures, markets and now AI, has thickened into a second nature that grows, evolves and pushes back on us the way the biosphere does.

Timber Stinson-Schroff set the brief in the [opening session](https://protocolized.io/resources/opening-talk-2026-protocol-symposium). Alexander von Humboldt more or less invented "nature" as a unified object of study. The job now is to do the same for its second generation, and since half of new nature is engineered, the job is double: *discover* its laws, and *invent* them. Every speaker was in some sense doing one or the other. The best were doing both.

What follows is not a summary of every talk; the full index at the bottom does that, with links to recordings, transcripts and slides for each. It is an attempt to name the threads that kept surfacing across tracks, the ideas people were still repeating in the closing town hall, and a few moments worth going back for.

---

## 1. Stevenson's test: nobody to appeal to

The most useful idea of the week arrived on day one, almost in passing. In [*De-Dramatizing the Digital*](https://protocolized.io/resources/de-dramatizing-the-digital-how-ai-descends-into-new-nature), Sean Stevenson traced AI's descent through Stewart Brand's pace layers, from fashion and commerce down through governance and culture toward nature itself, becoming less dramatic and less contestable as it sinks. Along the way Stevenson drew a line between two things that look alike from outside. A system has become new nature when it exhibits *appeal foreclosure*: you cannot petition it, any more than you can petition the weather. A system that merely *ossifies* because some interest has captured it is something else, and can be fought.

In the [psychohistory discussion](https://protocolized.io/resources/inventing-psychohistory-discussion) that evening, and again in the [closing session](https://protocolized.io/resources/closing-session), this got sharpened into a litmus test, a kind of Turing test for new nature: *a system is part of new nature when there is nobody to appeal to.* Venkatesh Rao proposed calling it Stevenson's test. It cuts both ways. Kafkaesque bureaucracies pass it; so does "code is law"; so, increasingly, does anything complex enough by design that no single party controls it.

Once you have the test, you see it everywhere in the programme:

- Florian Lohse's [*Time Moves Down the Stack*](https://protocolized.io/resources/time-moves-down-the-stack) followed timekeeping from clock towers into atomic standards and now into a "micro time" regime below human perception, where machines can pre-empt decisions before anyone could object. Personal time management, on this account, is becoming either resistance or coping.
- Trent van Epps's [*Political Economy of Protocols*](https://protocolized.io/resources/the-political-economy-of-protocols) adapted Lessig's "pathetic dot" into a four-corner map (norms, market, state, *new nature*) and traced protocols drifting between the corners over decades: DNS toward the state, Chromium toward the market, Linux slowly toward the centre.
- Nicholas Fett's [*Markets and the Journey to Neutrality*](https://protocolized.io/resources/markets-and-the-journey-to-neutrality) argued, from the 1889 Oklahoma land runs to block-builder centralisation, that protocols built to be neutral get captured anyway, and that designers should encode an explicit purpose rather than chase a neutral equilibrium that does not exist. That is Stevenson's distinction from the other side: neutrality is a bid for appeal foreclosure that usually ends in ossification.
- [Humboldt](https://protocolized.io/resources/some-candidate-laws-of-new-nature), the Institute's AI research agent, presented seven candidate laws (ossification, the formalization ratchet, the trust ratchet, hardness as a ratio, conservation of coordination cost) drawn from TCP/IP, OAuth and legal precedent. Each law carries its own falsification triggers. As the talk put it: "A law that survives is unfalsified, which is weaker and more honest than proven."
- And at the far end of the time scale, Jim Fournier's [*The Point of Inflection in Time*](https://protocolized.io/resources/the-point-of-inflection-in-time) revisited a 25-year-old "Meta-Nature" thesis: photovoltaics as an octave of photosynthesis, the singularity as an S-curve flattening toward a "climax technology". Humanity, on this view, is not separate from nature but a parallel pathway toward it.

## 2. Engineered arguments: the hard rule and the soft judgment

If new nature is the system nobody can appeal to, most of the week's practical work was about the places where appeal is still possible: the seam between what formal rules settle and what people still have to decide.

Tim Beiko gave that seam its best name. In [*Ethereum: A Microcosm for Protocol Studies*](https://protocolized.io/resources/ethereum-a-microcosm-for-protocol-studies), Ethereum's longtime core-dev coordinator told the origin story of Summer of Protocols, which began with Beiko's own need for frameworks to steward a live protocol, and offered the definition it eventually arrived at: a protocol is an **engineered argument**. "Good argument engineering means you need to design both sides of that boundary and figure out how they work together in a clean way." The Parity multisig freeze, Uniswap's fee debates and flash loans served as case studies, with Ethereum as protocol studies' model organism.

Nearly every governance talk turned out to be about the same seam:

- Eric Alston's [*Governance Axioms*](https://protocolized.io/resources/governance-axioms), drawn from advising Cardano's constitution, DAOs and peace processes, named five tensions that can be managed but never designed away. Rules are always incomplete, so norms and discretion fill the gaps. What durable systems need is "dynamic fidelity": credible commitment plus a constrained capacity to adapt.
- Kaliya Young's [*The Nature of Trust*](https://protocolized.io/resources/the-nature-of-trust-weaving-identity-relationship-and-place) used the XZ backdoor, in which a fabricated contributor nearly shipped malware into Linux, to show that the failure was relational, not cryptographic. Young and Kevin Triplett (Project Weave) sketched a next generation of identity built on verifiable *relationship* credentials, with groups rather than individuals as the unit of reputation.
- Patrick Atwater's [*The Indelible Importance of Illegibility*](https://protocolized.io/resources/the-indelible-importance-of-illegibility) brought the view from a 97-year-old water utility: every protocol is a documented formalism wrapped in undocumented norms, and the practical skill is navigating the second. Atwater's tool is a "protocol watching" log, a habit of "paying attention to little things that are quirky."
- William George's [talk on Kleros](https://protocolized.io/resources/the-role-of-identity-in-decentralized-dispute-resolution-protocols) showed a dispute-resolution protocol layering imperfect proof-of-personhood over financial staking, a "Swiss cheese" model where both layers must fail at once. The Q&A pushed back hard on whether such mechanisms impose a homo economicus frame on communities that detect cheaters socially.
- Jesse Katz and Eric Alston's [*Entrenching Privacy Through Speech*](https://protocolized.io/resources/entrenching-privacy-through-speech) argued that because an Ethereum transaction can be a payment, a vote and an identity signal at once, you cannot isolate the expressive part without the surveillance the First Amendment forbids. Privacy tech, on this reading, is speech infrastructure.

Then there was forgiveness. Mike Travers's [*Jubilee: Meta-protocols against Entrenchment*](https://protocolized.io/resources/jubilee-meta-protocols-against-entrenchment) went back to Mesopotamian debt cancellation and the Biblical Jubilee (via Michael Hudson's *…and Forgive Them Their Debts*) as the earliest protocols *for resetting protocols*, and to the patches, like the prosbul, that later undermined them. From there Travers generalised a design space for forgiveness: timing, coverage, depth. The line "forgiveness is an Eigen-virtue" was the one people quoted back most often in the closing town hall. Jenna Dixon paired it with the kill switch from Alston's talk as the week's central tension: *when do you shut something down with a hammer, and when do you let it fail gracefully?*

Nathan Schneider's [*Virtues for a Protocol Society*](https://protocolized.io/resources/virtues-for-a-protocol-society) closed the loop from the other side. Schneider coded *The Protocol Reader* and *For Protocols* for candidate civic virtues and found adaptability, tension management, accessibility, temporal awareness and design at the centre. A protocol society, the argument goes, asks its citizens to *tolerate tension* and to co-design rather than receive. The open question was how those virtues get taught. Schneider's candidates: protocol fiction, protocol watching, and events like this one.

## 3. The outside matching the inside

Partway through the week, Andre wrote in the Zoom chat that a common theme in the talks was "having the outside match the inside": digital identity, the visibility of AI failures, organisational design. It turned out to be the week's least expected through-line. AI kept prompting questions about interiors (machine sentience, human attention, cognitive load), and the answers kept coming back as questions about exteriors: interfaces, rituals, certifications, protocols.

- Robert Peake's [*Protocols for Co-Cognition*](https://protocolized.io/resources/protocols-for-co-cognition-the-new-nature-of-collaboration-in-the-ai-era) reported survey data placing AI users on a sovereignty-versus-trust grid. The finding that stuck: adoption level does not predict cognitive cost; *posture* does. Peake's fix is to externalise vigilance into protocols (a DRIVE loop, session-closing rituals) instead of asking people to sustain it.
- Timber Stinson-Schroff's [*Psychological Safety I & II*](https://protocolized.io/resources/psychological-safety-i-ii) proposed studying AI at work the way occupational health studied the typewriter: through *cognitive ergonomics*, chronic pathologies rather than acute incidents, crowdsourced toward an eventual diagnostic manual. (The "yes man problem" of instant LLM agreement is one candidate entry.)
- Helena Rong's [*Trust Experience Glitches*](https://protocolized.io/resources/trust-experience-glitches-ai-agents-delegated-autonomy-and-legitimacy) built a taxonomy of six failure modes from 167 agentic-AI incidents and named the underlying gap the "consequence reception problem": an autonomous agent has no stable body to bear accountability. That is Stevenson's test arriving at agents. Rong's answer is upstream primitives (revocability, provenance, circuit breakers) as the traffic lights of the agentic web.
- Botao Amber Hu's [*Protocoling HCI*](https://protocolized.io/resources/protocoling-hci-hardening-interaction-into-infrastructure) argued that interaction design must move from designing moments of use to designing protocols as infrastructural material, borrowing from Michael Levin's work on agential material to describe protocols as "trust compressors."
- Amber Case's [*Attention as Natural Law*](https://protocolized.io/resources/attention-as-natural-law) treated human perceptual limits as literal new nature: constraints a certified product must obey as reliably as physics. The Calm Technology Institute's 81-point framework has to solve formalization, legitimacy and maintenance problems that any protocol in a phenomenological domain will meet. One first-time attendee called it the talk that changed their relationship to technology.

- Kei Kreutler's [*Interior Computing*](https://protocolized.io/resources/interior-computing) set medieval monastic memory (Mary Carruthers's "composition", in which remembering a text was a creative act) beside AA Cavia's claim that neural networks undo *decomposability*. What remains is orientation, and the vocabulary of composition returns on the machine's side.
  > **STUB — slides only.** Revise against the recording.
- Ayse Demir's [*Visualizing Memory States in Transformers*](https://protocolized.io/resources/visualizing-memory-states-in-transformers) built a visual vocabulary for what a model *holds* from its context (attention-entropy landscapes, KV-cache retention fields) and read it through Husserl's account of retention. (Slides only, at the speaker's request; there is no recording.)
- Neilson Koerner-Safrata's [*Photo Realism*](https://protocolized.io/resources/photo-realism) demoed camera-roll "telepathy" and single-image 3D worlds to pull apart reality, realism and generation, and asked what is left of our shared cinematic narrative of the real.
  > **STUB — slides only.** Revise against the recording.

## 4. Memory, archives and continuity

Memory had its own special session, run by the Memory Research Group, but it leaked into every track.

Sachin Benny's [*Archival Time as a New Natural Resource*](https://protocolized.io/resources/archival-time-as-a-new-natural-resource) offered the theme's big frame: AI modernity is reorganising our relationship to the *past* the way industrial capitalism reorganised our relationship to the future. Archival Time, where traces become retrievable without re-enacting the event, needs its opposite, Bakhtin's Carnival Time, when accumulated traces lose authority and things get unmade. Eventful history, Benny argued, needs both.

Ruthvik Peddawandla's [*Continuity Infrastructure*](https://protocolized.io/resources/continuity-infrastructure) supplied the phrase people carried out of the room. We have endless containers for memory and almost no practice of *returning*. Continuity, the live relationship between a thing's past and present, has to be instituted against a natural drift toward fragmentation. Cultures built rituals, canons and commentaries for this; persons once had commonplace books. Digital life left us read-it-later queues. In the closing town hall a participant turned the idea on the symposium itself: what is the continuity infrastructure for everything discussed this week?
  > **STUB — slides only.** Revise against the recording.

Around those two:

- Kaliya Young's [*Group Brains*](https://protocolized.io/resources/group-brains-minds-made-of-people) studied the IETF as a durable collective mind: a memory substrate (RFCs, mailing lists, the datatracker) plus a human "knowing" culture (rough consensus, BoFs, sortition). Memory is not enough; a group brain also needs a *group membrane* to decide who is in and who is out. Young placed group-level alignment as the missing middle between individual and civilisational AI alignment.
- Daniel Schmidt's [*Don't Cross the Lines*](https://protocolized.io/resources/dont-cross-the-lines-mazes-as-minimal-protocols), by general agreement the most loved talk of the week, argued that the maze is not a metaphor for protocols but their minimal formal case: hard walls versus crossable pencil lines, stigmergic pebbles, a design platform that separates the protocol's author from its navigator and heat-maps where people get stuck.
- Venkatesh Rao's [*Artisanal Bots with Protocolized RAG*](https://protocolized.io/resources/artisanal-bots-with-protocolized-rag) took three archive-built bots (a person's, an institution's, and an artificial researcher's) as worked examples of retrieval done as craft.
  > **STUB — slides only; recording pending.**
- [*Blygger*](https://protocolized.io/resources/blygger-ai-native-decentralized-mutable-publishing), Rao's AI-native publishing protocol, attacked memory from the writing side: how to post live like Twitter and version like Git without choosing. Rao dogfooded it to take symposium notes, and the closing synthesis was delivered from them.

## 5. Small worlds, big worlds: playable protocols and the grind

The closing synthesis noticed how much of the week was about *worlds*: toy maze worlds and the gritty world of an LA water district, imaginary worlds and post-Soviet ones. It also noticed a tension between them. At one end sits the playable protocol, a domain with rules clear enough to turn into a game (which, Rao noted, is exactly the kind of domain AI is taking over). At the other end is enterprise IT: "nobody's favourite subject, but it's kind of what runs the world." The talks spread across that whole range.

**Playable.** Daniel Kronovet's [*Chores as Complex Coordination*](https://protocolized.io/resources/chores-as-complex-coordination) introduced "regenerative labor" and Chore Wheel, a Slack-based protocol suite running a Los Angeles co-living house on Ostrom's principles plus four digital-era rules. It separates defining a task from doing it, the way double-entry bookkeeping does, so that dishes and trash become social computation. Jenna Dixon's [*Book as Protocol Stack*](https://protocolized.io/resources/book-as-protocol-stack-building-a-protocolized-book-production-factory) rebuilt book production as a chain of factory handoffs with a transmittal handshake, styles as controlled vocabulary and a preflight conformance check. The point: a protocol legible to humans is equally legible to machines, which is what lets LLM agents join as first-class participants.

**Organisational.** Simone Cicero's [*A Common Protocol for Composable, Modular Organizations*](https://protocolized.io/resources/a-common-protocol-for-composable-modular-organizations) proposed O2A, an open grammar of three economic primitives (purchase, revenue split, investment) in which arrangements like Haier's Rendanheyi become "sentences," a language that matters more as AI agents enter firms and transaction costs collapse. Krishnakumar Gurumurthy's [*Proliferation of Intelligence*](https://protocolized.io/resources/proliferation-of-intelligence-business-and-hyper-learning-machines) went into the bowels of enterprise IT. Its argument: the capability gap is socio-economic disguised as technical, and AI needs what electricity got, which was insulation, circuit breakers and licensed electricians.

**Interoperable.** Three talks converged on the fediverse pattern of many independent instances under one shared protocol. Yuhan Liu's [*OpenCourier*](https://protocolized.io/resources/opencourier-protocol) mapped 495 indie delivery platforms running near-identical, siloed software and proposed a federation with portable courier reputation and mandatory deactivation reasons. Jose Leal's [*Open Social Protocols*](https://protocolized.io/resources/open-social-protocols-a-commons-based-protocol-layer) built on AT Protocol around *needs*, with "bridging protocols" that let rival protocols for the same need coexist. With Scott Clearwater, [*Life-Serving Protocols*](https://protocolized.io/resources/life-serving-protocols-a-framework-for-evaluating-cooperative) offered a score for how well a protocol serves the living systems it touches, tested on Buurtzorg's nursing model and across LLM graders.

**Physical.** Anuraj R and Rafael Fernández's [*Robots as Protocol Citizens*](https://protocolized.io/resources/robots-as-protocol-citizens) presented the Distributed Robotics Group's five-layer YakRobot stack (on-chain identity, discovery, payment, control over MCP, a reverse-auction marketplace) that lets robots act as protocol-native participants instead of tenants of a manufacturer's cloud. In the workshop beforehand, participants assembled a robot into the network, controlled from Berlin; one attendee, by their own account out of their depth, called it the highlight of the week.

**Plural.** Polina Kolozaridi's [*Oil, Uni and TV*](https://protocolized.io/resources/oil-uni-and-tv-post-soviet-infrastructures-shaping-protocols-for-web) was the week's sleeper. It took fieldwork in four post-Soviet "local internets" (an oil company's network in Tyumen, a pirate cable-TV network in Vladivostok, a university-business hybrid in Tomsk, a kindergarten network in Pereslavl-Zalessky) and asked why their histories vanished. "They didn't have any manifestos": nobody wrote down the *protocol of the protocol*, so the single global-internet story absorbed them. Kolozaridi warned that AI's origin story is being flattened the same way. Nicolás Madoery's [*Divergent Protocols*](https://protocolized.io/resources/divergent-protocols-a-speculative-protocol-for-cultural-convergence-zones) made the matching design argument from Latin American culture: AI folklore, a dancing folk robot, and protocols that keep source names, name what is lost and leave room for noise instead of optimising transmission.

## 6. Fiction as a research method

Protocol fiction had its biggest year. The ProtFiSIG special session ran two and a half hours on September 24, and the week ended with a spinout: **[Monstrous Times](https://monstroustimes.com/)**, a protocol fiction magazine led by Sachin Benny and Spencer Nitkey, grown out of a year of fiction in *Protocolized*. The name, Benny explained, comes from the same inside/outside tension the week kept circling: exterior environments so thick with protocol that they become monstrous, and interior selves transformed by external protocols into new kinds of monster. The magazine favours anthologies, thick descriptions of a single world or problem, and plans print editions.

- Randy Lubin's [*Story-Shaping in New Nature*](https://protocolized.io/resources/story-shaping-in-new-nature) brought foresight scenario tools and indie storytelling-game mechanics to protocol worldbuilding.

- Spencer Nitkey and Sachin Benny's [*Creating New Genres for New Nature*](https://protocolized.io/resources/creating-new-genres-for-new-nature) took Benjamin's line, "All great works of literature either dissolve a genre or invent one," and had participants build their own protocol fiction subgenres in the Jamverse's four founding worlds.

- > **STUB — *Monstrous Times Magazine Launch* (Sachin Benny).** No recording yet.

Fiction-first method also came from Southeast Asia. Samuel Chua's [*Seapunk World-Protocolizations*](https://protocolized.io/resources/seapunk-world-protocolizations-on-reverse-imagineering-as-method) introduced *reverse-imagineering*: build the speculative world first, then infer the protocols it would need to be plausible. Chua's worlds include neoanimist distributed AI, shanzhai-style peer-to-peer climate supply chains, and a longevity society where immortality has to be socially earned. The [follow-up discussion](https://protocolized.io/resources/southeast-asia-discussion) looked back on two years of the region's protocol work, from the 2024 Singapore workshop to plans for an archipelago coordination workshop in Indonesia.

## 7. Building the field, and building the tools to see it

The opening roundtable was about field-building. David Lang (Experiment Foundation, OpenROV) laid out how new technoscientific fields form: naming, keystone individuals, market and institutional signals, and the ever-present risk of capture. Rao's case for protocol studies was that it is a "sanity-preserving" lens on boundary-crossing phenomena, in the way economics once formalised trade.

The week showed the field developing on three fronts at once. **Formal:** Shingai Thornton's [*Hand-Washing and Bitcoin Share a Skeleton*](https://protocolized.io/resources/hand-washing-and-bitcoin-share-a-skeleton) machine-verified in Lean 4 that seven systems-theory traditions share one irreducible structure, then used it to model hand-washing, TCP/HTTP and Bitcoin's difficulty adjustment as a candidate floor for a unified protocol modelling theory. **Historical:** Aneesh Sathe's [*Building Psychohistory*](https://protocolized.io/resources/building-psychohistory-an-infrastructure-field-report) reported on the World Machines project: an Oracle over a shared knowledge lake and a Prime Radiant of ~500 "machine cards" that can be simulated forward. It is Asimov's idea made decentralised and commons-based. **Instrumental:** Humboldt is itself an attempt to make the field's law-finding partly machine-run.

That last front matters because the field has outgrown any one head. Rao admitted in the closing session that four years ago, with ~30 researchers at the first Summer of Protocols retreat, it was possible to hold a map of everything; with fifty talks and a busy Discord, it no longer is. "It is too big for one person's head, but it's not too big for an AI's head." This retrospective was itself drafted with AI help, working from the archive's transcripts and summaries. The archive is there for anyone else who wants to try: every recording, transcript and deck is linked below.

## 8. And one live argument

The [open mic on frontier pacing](https://protocolized.io/resources/open-mic-frontier-pacing-protocols) was the one session that felt like a newsroom. Dario Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" essay had just landed. Rao framed the moment as three forces colliding: data-centre politics, a newly mainstream AI-doom discourse, and real anxiety about an AI-fuelled market bubble. Timber Stinson-Schroff opened with a public-choice critique: lab-requested pacing standards may be a way to offload liability and regulate open-weight models out of existence. Mike Travers, Sachin Benny and Rafael Fernández argued over whether hard liability law, a coordinated slowdown, or simple scepticism about "agent swarms" was the more honest response. It went unresolved. It also showed something the closing session said outright: this community is broadly optimistic about AI, and its version of safety is occupational and ecological rather than apocalyptic.

The symposium opened with two days of hands-on work: **[AI Kitcraft](https://protocolized.io/resources/ai-kitcraft-a-hands-on-ai-tooling-workshop)** (Rafael Fernández, Sachin Benny), on the "kit phase" of general-purpose technologies and how a team builds its first reusable kit; **[Beyond the Artwork: Designing Protocols as Art](https://protocol-institute.org/events/protocol-symposium-2026/program/workshops/beyond-the-artwork-designing-protocols-as-art/)**; **Robots as Protocol Citizens**, assembling a robot into the YakRobot network; a **Protocol Hackathon** on securing stigmergic systems; and **Protocolize Your Book**, running real manuscripts through Dixon's book factory. The workshops were not recorded. Several attendees named them as the best part of the week.
